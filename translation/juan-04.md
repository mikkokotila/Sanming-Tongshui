# Sanming Tonghui · Juan Four
## A Comprehensive Gathering of the Three Fates

**Compiled by Wan Minying of the Ming dynasty.** From the imperially commissioned *Siku Quanshu*.[^01]

*Reading conventions.* **Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water** name the Five Phases. *Qi*, *yin*, *yang*, *nayin*, and **Lu** retain their technical meanings. Stem–branch names are given in unaccented pinyin. Angled brackets **〈 〉** distinguish the source's small-character commentary; square brackets identify supplied clarification or a textual repair. Capitalized names such as Birth, Death, Extinction, Officer, and Seal are technical terms, not ordinary descriptions in every occurrence. Endnotes explain changes of reference, textual variants, and unresolved readings. The Chinese source has not been altered.[^02]

## 1. On the Ten Stems Seated upon Their Branches, with Month, Hour, and the Good and Ill of the Luck Course
### 論十干坐支兼得月時及行運吉㓙

<!-- source: L14-L24 -->
#### Jia and Yi 甲乙

**Jia Wood** belongs to yang: it is timber for pillars and beams. It welcomes birth in autumn or winter; encountering a Shen or Zi month is auspicious. Geng or Xin in the pillars is understood as axe and chisel, indicating reputation and profit. A luck course through Shen, You, Chen, Xu, Chou, or Wei can bring great advancement; Xin as Officer is especially fine. A Yin–Wu–Xu combination forming a configuration, or exposed Ding Fire as Hurting Officer, is avoided: this makes a fate of toil and effort, accomplishing nothing. Meeting these in the luck course is also unfavorable. Yet if that configuration forms and Ding is exposed, while the pillars contain Chen, Xu, Chou, or Wei and Wu or Ji appears in the stems, a subsequent Wealth course allows Hurting Officer to generate Wealth, and great blessing unfolds instead.[^03]

**Yi Wood** belongs to yin: it is Wood full of living qi. Born in spring, its flowers and leaves flourish; it also welcomes the season of Little Spring. Meeting the Hai–Mao–Wei or Shen–Zi–Chen configuration and then traveling a northern course, it is unharmed even by exposed Bing, Ding, Geng, or Xin. It avoids Yin–Wu–Xu Fire and Si–You–Chou Metal, which often bring injury. A further southern course indicates early death without doubt.[^03]
<!-- source: L26-L36 -->
> For Jia and Yi, distinction lies in Wood finding its proper condition;  
> Know that flourishing Metal and Water make the exceptional.  
> From spring go south; from autumn return north;  
> In winter and summer, westward travel lays blessing's foundation.

People born on Jia or Yi days, seated in Si, You, Chou, Shen, or Xu—the region of Metal—favor luck courses through the domains of Earth and Metal. If born in Yin, Mao, or Chen without a Wood configuration forming, they gain great distinction when the hour leads them into the Earth–Metal domain. The luck course is judged likewise, and their office endures. If born in Si, You, Chou, or Shen, distinction is obtained when the hour leads them into Hai, Mao, Wei, or Yin. Without such an hour, there is excess or deficiency: a luck course through the Water–Wood configuration's domain is then needed, or they remain impoverished scholars. When Wealth stars were originally present in the pillars, Companions and Rob Wealth dividing and seizing them are feared; without original Wealth, this fear does not arise.

Wood obtains Metal and becomes a useful vessel: the humane person possesses courage. Metal obtains Wood and becomes useful material: the courageous person must possess humanity. This is hardness and softness assisting one another, yin and yang evenly balanced; the luck course then welcomes Wealth and Officer. Wood without Metal leaves Geng and Xin deficient and righteousness scant. Metal without Fire gives courage without propriety, and so disorder. Excessive Metal without Water withers; excessive Wood without Metal grows rank. Thus Metal and Wood do not follow one undifferentiated rule. One-sided yin or yang makes a fate difficult to classify: even Wealth and Officer do not bring advancement.

〈The poem on the six Jia days says:〉[^04]

**“Established Lu at Yin is flourishing ground.”** 〈At Yin, Jia Wood sits upon Lu. Metal is extinct and Earth dead: Wealth and Officer both turn away. A Xinwei hour brings distinction.〉 **“In autumn, facing the Messenger, the ghost inflicts injury.”** 〈Within Shen, Jia is extinct and Geng is Killings; in autumn birth, the ghost flourishes.〉 **“Seated beside Lu in Xu, the heart cherishes goodness.”** 〈Xu holds Xin's remaining qi and Wu Earth's proper position: the body sits upon Wealth and Officer. Though the body is burned by Fire, the heart often cherishes goodness. A Bingyin hour brings distinction.〉 **“Wealth hidden at Chen likewise makes a good disposition.”** 〈Wu and Ji enter Tomb within Chen. The body sits upon the Wealth Storehouse; Water qi begins to grow, and the disposition is often kindly. A Bingyin hour brings distinction.〉 **“Wu welcomes Ji Wealth: Heaven grants pardon.”** 〈Ji Earth is established and flourishing within Wu; Ding Fire hurts Officer. There is Wealth but no Officer. Summer birth makes Heavenly Pardon.〉 **“Though Zi is Bathing, the day need cause no harm.”** 〈Jia Wood at Zi is indeed Bathing, but Gui's Lu flourishes within Zi: the seat provides generating qi and Seal-Ribbon. Winter birth makes Heavenly Pardon.〉

> The auspicious becomes ominous, the ominous auspicious:  
> Examine Heaven's season again, and judge with care.

The six Jia days use Xin as Direct Officer, Geng as Indirect Officer, and Wu and Ji as Wealth. If Wu, Ji, and Xin emerge in year, month, or hour, with birth in the three autumn months, the four season-ending months, or a Metal–Earth configuration, Wealth and Officer are useful. Even without those three stems emerging, birth in those seasons or configurations is still judged through Wealth and Officer. Jia or Yi seizing Wealth, or Bing and Ding injuring Officer, makes reputation and profit difficult. In spring, summer, or a Fire–Wood configuration, Wealth and Officer lack qi: even assistance gives them little weight. A westward course or the Earth–Metal domains of the four season-ending months is welcome, approaching Officer and arriving at Wealth. The southeastern Wood–Fire region, which injures Officer and defeats Wealth, is unwelcome.

If both Geng and Xin appear in the Four Pillars, this is **Officer and Killings mixed together**. Unless one is removed and the other retained, or restraint is provided, poverty and low status follow instead. If Geng alone appears without restraint, judge it as a **ghost**. Distinguish the strength of body and ghost to determine good and ill, longevity and early death. With restraint in due measure, it is judged as Indirect Officer; excessive restraint, however, does not bring blessing. Examine further whether the day stem has strength and assistance within its birth month, distinguishing the depth of the solar term and the relative weight of each influence. A course strengthening the body while weakening the ghost is welcome; one weakening the body while strengthening the ghost is avoided.[^05]

The six Yi days use Wu as Direct Wealth, Ji as Indirect Wealth, Geng as Direct Officer, and Xin as Indirect Officer. In spring, summer, or a Fire–Wood configuration, even existing Wealth and Officer lack qi, and assistance gives them little weight. A western course through Earth and Metal, approaching Officer and arriving at Wealth, is welcome. Fire–Wood territory, which injures Officer and defeats Wealth, is avoided. #### Bing and Ding 丙丁

**Bing Fire** belongs to yang: it is the sun's proper qi, capable of generating the myriad things. It welcomes birth in spring or summer, when it comes naturally into its own and its vitality is a hundredfold. Heavenly and Monthly Virtue together with an eastern luck course are excellent; even Ren or Gui Water does no harm. Only exposed Wu Earth is avoided, diminishing its measure. If the major course and annual ruler bring the offending influence, there are official proceedings, imprisonment, loss of wealth, and mourning. Born in autumn or winter, at night, with a Water configuration additionally formed by the branches, it makes a servant or dependent: a life of separation, solitude, poverty, premature death, or disability.[^06]

**Ding Fire** belongs to yin: it is ordinary fire, capable of working upon all things. Gold, silver, copper, and iron cannot become useful vessels without Ding's working. It welcomes night birth; Si, You, or Chou as monthly command is excellent. In the first month, Yin supplies Heavenly Virtue and the Seal source; Mao in addition is best. Ren and Gui Water are avoided. Daytime birth often harms wife and children. A southern course strips office and removes appointment; a northwestern course brings distinction.

> Bing and Ding day rulers have Fire for their root;  
> Metal and Water, the two stars, are blessing's source.  
> If the luck course reaches west and north,  
> Even wealth and distinction are not wholly secure.[^06]

Bing and Ding days seated in Shen, Zi, Chen, or Hai—the Water positions—and further led into a Metal hour, with birth in Yin, Wu, or Si, form **Water and Fire in Completion**, bringing great distinction. In the fifth month of summer, a triple Fire configuration is avoided: Fire blazes and Water dries up. In winter's Zi month, a triple Water configuration is avoided: Water abounds and Fire is extinguished. Only their balance brings Completion. The major course favors Metal–Water domains, but avoids both excess and deficiency. One-sided yin or yang produces seedlings that never come into bloom. With birth in Shen, Zi, Chen, or Hai, a Yin, Wu, Xu, or Si hour is needed for distinction. Without such an hour, only a Wood course is favorable; otherwise there is an empty name, not distinction.[^07]

Guisi and Gengyin hours bring distinction.〉 **“At Chen, Officer's Storehouse is avoided in winter birth.”** 〈The body sits in Officer's region at Chen, where Ren and Gui enter Tomb. The body stands beside Wealth's region; with summer birth, Wealth and Officer lack qi.〉[^08]

> Waxing, waning, fullness, emptiness—their principle is subtle;  
> Master rest and flourishing before discussing service or withdrawal.

The six Bing days use Geng and Xin as Wealth, Gui as Direct Officer, and Ren as Indirect Officer. If Geng, Xin, and Gui emerge in year, month, or hour, with birth in autumn, winter, or a Metal–Water configuration, Wealth and Officer are useful. Even without those three stems emerging, those seasons or configurations are still judged through Wealth and Officer. Bing or Ding seizing Wealth, or Ji as Hurting Officer, makes reputation and profit difficult. In summer, the four season-ending months, or Fire–Earth configurations, even existing Wealth and Officer lack qi: assistance gives them little weight. A northwestern course through Metal and Water, approaching Officer and arriving at Wealth, is welcome. Ren and Gui both present make mixed Officer and Killings; without restraint, low status follows. Ren without Gui and without restraint is judged as a ghost. Determine the strength of body and ghost to judge good and ill, longevity and early death. Restraint in due measure allows its use as Indirect Officer; excessive restraint does not bring blessing. Examine whether the day stem has strength and rescue within its birth month, distinguishing depth within the solar term and relative weight. A course strengthening the body while weakening the ghost is welcome; one weakening the body while strengthening the ghost is avoided.

#### Wu and Ji 戊己

**Wu Earth** belongs to yang: it is the earth of embankments and city walls, able to hold back Water but not to plant and nourish the myriad things. When walls and embankments suffer no punishment, clash, breaking, or harm, the people are secure. It welcomes Jia and Yi Wood where Killings transforms into Seal. Western luck courses are avoided: even advancement must bring loss and anxiety. Fire's generation and assistance are needed; Water's control and restraint are disliked. Repeated Wu and Ji lose both reputation and profit; repeated Xin and Geng make undertakings advance and retreat.[^09]

**Ji Earth** belongs to yin: it is the earth of fields, hills, and gardens, capable of planting and nourishing all things. It needs punishment, clash, breaking, and harm, understood as ploughing and digging. It welcomes spring or summer birth, in the regions of Chen and Si where Officer and Seal lie. If nothing further injures Officer or damages Seal, blessing develops. The person likes establishing fields and gardens and enjoys abundance. Northeastern luck courses are especially fine. With Hai, Mao, and Wei Wood as well, wealth and distinction are certain: the person is steady and substantial, generous in great matters but impatient in small ones. Meeting Chen, Xu, Chou, and Wei makes Turning Away from Lu and Pursuing Horse, together with Rob Wealth, injury, losses, lawsuits, and mourning in many forms.

> Wu and Ji day stems seek Water and Wood;  
> Already within the pillars, these become blessing.  
> Luck reaching northern country and the east:  
> Virtue enriches the person, wealth enriches the house.

Wu and Ji days seated upon Hai, Mao, or Yin form **Gouchen Obtaining Its Position**. Luck courses favor Water–Wood domains. Birth in Hai or Zi requires the hour to lead into Chen, Xu, Chou, Wei, Si, or Wu. Birth in those Earth or Fire months requires a Hai or Zi hour for distinction. Earth obtains Wood and is opened and loosened; Wood relies upon Earth for cultivation. Too much Wood and too little Earth causes collapse. Too much Earth without Wood is stubborn, turbid, useless earth. For a Ji day with Chou in year and month, the west is inauspicious; the south brings conspicuous distinction.[^09]

If Ren, Gui, and Yi emerge in year, month, or hour, with birth in spring, winter, or a Water–Wood configuration, Wealth and Officer are useful. Even without those three emerging, birth in those conditions is still judged through Wealth and Officer. Wu or Ji seizing Wealth, or Xin injuring Officer, makes reputation and profit difficult. With birth in autumn, the four season-ending months, or Metal–Earth configurations, Wealth and Officer lack qi, and assistance gives them little weight. Distinguish body and ghost in strength to judge good and ill, longevity and early death. Restraint in balanced measure allows its use as Indirect Officer; excessive restraint gives no blessing. Examine the day stem's strength and rescue within the birth month, the depth within the solar term, and relative weight. Welcome a strong-body, weak-ghost course; avoid a weak-body, strong-ghost course.

#### Geng and Xin 庚辛

**Geng Metal** belongs to yang: it is gold, silver, copper, iron, and their kind, formed by receiving the sun's influence. It needs Ding Fire's working before it can become a useful vessel. Meeting Bing Fire is as though not meeting it. Southeastern courses through Fire and Wood are welcome: Metal is made bright through working. Yin and Mao under Jia and Yi, and Si, Wu, and Wei where Officer Star and the Seal source obtain qi, all bring advancement. Only in the northwest does Metal sink to the bottom of Water, unable to become a useful vessel.

**Xin Metal** belongs to yin: it is quicksilver, cinnabar, red and blue-green precious substances, pearls, and their kind, formed from the sun's essence, the moon's radiance, and refined qi. Above all it needs clear Metal, refined Water, and a domain rich in Earth qi, together with a northwestern course. If the course goes through Chen, Xu, or Si toward the southeast, the absence of Ding Fire from the Four Pillars is best. With Ding present, it cannot be made useful: like a pearl dropped into a furnace, it is refined but bears no fruit. A Yin–Wu–Xu configuration is especially feared. When Killings flourishes, the body must be strong enough to withstand it. Yet if the pillars contain Hai, Mao, and Wei and Bing and Ding are exposed, a Wu–Wei course unfolds blessing. Si–You–Chou forming Metal makes a warm and substantial pattern of transformation: an eastern course is greatly auspicious, a southern one unsuitable.[^11]

> Geng and Xin day rulers are named Metal stems;  
> Wood and Fire generating one another make blessing their own.  
> If year, month, and hour meet in combination,  
> Courses east and west assuredly bring office.

Geng and Xin days seated upon Yin, Wu, Xu, or Si Fire, with birth also in Yin, Wu, or Xu, require a Metal or Earth hour for distinction. In the three autumn months, late winter, or the eleventh month, an hour leading into flourishing Wood and Fire brings great distinction. The luck course follows Wood–Fire domains. Excess and deficiency are avoided: one-sided yang or yin produces seedlings that never flower. If the month's qi connects with Fire, distinction requires a Si, You, Chou, or Shen hour; Metal–Earth luck is auspicious. If Companions and triple combination form a Metal configuration, with Metal abundant and Fire slight, Wood–Fire courses are welcome.

Metal without Fire cannot become a useful vessel; Fire without Metal has nothing through which to display its function. Metal and Fire in balance alone bring carriage and ceremonial cap. If Fire blazes excessively without Earth, Metal must fail; with Earth, the image is **Casting the Seal**. Through the furnace's working and transformative refinement it becomes useful: a great person's fate. Much Fire and little Metal, or abundant Metal and slight Fire, instead make violent people.

## 3. On the Five Phases in Their Seasons, Places, and Regional Domains
### 論五行時地分野吉㓙

<!-- source: L260-L262 -->
Consider Master Wang's account. The two qi are yin and yang; the Five Phases are Metal, Wood, Water, Fire, and Earth. Time means spring, summer, autumn, and winter; place means Ji, Qing, Yan, Xu, Yang, Jing, Liang, Yong, and Yu. Heaven has yin and yang moving through the four seasons; Earth has the Five Phases embodied in the Nine Provinces. This is precisely Master Zhu's saying: “The Five Phases' substance is complete upon Earth; their qi travels in Heaven.” Thus Heaven has spring, summer, autumn, and winter, while Earth has Metal, Wood, Water, and Fire: time and place work through one another.[^25]

Those who discuss fate today know only to discuss yin and yang and the Five Phases; they do not also consider directions, day and night, cloud and clear weather. Consequently, when people with the same year, month, day, and hour differ greatly in rank and lifespan, they declare the Five Phases groundless, encouraging the world's doubts about fate. This too is a distortion. Alas, human beings are born between Heaven and Earth and cannot escape the Five Phases. The Nine Provinces have their distinct boundaries; their winds and qi suit different conditions. Cloud and clarity, cold and warmth cannot reasonably obey one uniform measure. People are born receiving Heaven and Earth's numinous qi, yet what each receives at a single time differs. Thus rank and lifespan cannot be confined within the Eight Characters alone.

Take Jia, Yi, Yin, and Mao, which belong to Wood: birth in Yan or Qing obtains place; spring command obtains time. Bing, Ding, Si, and Wu belong to Fire: birth in Xu or Yang obtains place; summer command obtains time. Wu, Ji, Chen, Xu, Chou, and Wei belong to Earth: birth in Yu obtains place; the four season-ending months obtain time. Geng, Xin, Shen, and You belong to Metal: birth in Jing or Liang obtains place; autumn and winter obtain time. Ren, Gui, Hai, and Zi belong to Water: birth in Ji or Yong obtains place; winter command obtains time. Moreover, within day and night, cloud and clarity, there are cold and warmth; within yin and yang's creative transformation, preferences and aversions. Generation, control, restraint, transformation; checking and raising, lightness and weight—the subtlety lies in recognizing their changes, not clinging to one account.

#### On Wood 論木

In spring, Wood holds command. Clear weather spreads flowers and leaves; rain chills their shoots. Though the first month brings the harmonious meeting of three yangs, cold qi has not disappeared. Fire makes the impulse of life abundant, bringing incomparable wealth and distinction. Too much Fire drains the originating qi: people from Xu or Yang born by day become ill. Fire and Earth traveling together give both wealth and distinction; Earth without Fire provides no more than clothing and sustenance. Metal threatens breaking and injury, but Fire restraining it makes blessing. Water is not here mother and child generating one another: early spring's Wood has barely begun to live, and Water chills it, making the encounter unfavorable. Birth in Ji or Yong brings poverty and cold: men are dissolute and women promiscuous.[^26]

Second-month Wood grows freely and luxuriantly. Earth cultivating it is excellent; Fire and Earth traveling together bring wealth, distinction, and longevity. Abundant Fire still drains its qi, because the flowers have only begun to hold their blossoms while true yang disperses. People from Yan or Qing have wealth and distinction without anxiety; for those from Xu or Yang, the good is incomplete. Ren, Gui, Hai, or Zi in stem and branch is a fault. Water gives excessive generation, making the disposition unsettled and leading one away from ancestral home. Earth, Metal, and Water meeting bring early death without doubt, though poverty and low standing may accompany longevity. Metal cutting Wood injures it at its flourishing point, hewing down its living impulse. Is this Heaven and Earth's utmost humanity in generating things? This explains the earlier masters' saying that even flourishing spring Wood should not meet Metal.

In the third month, Wood extends freely and grows tall. Spring sunlight shines gently upon a thousand reds and ten thousand purples. Prolonged rain soaks it and shakes root and trunk. Earth gives deep roots and a firm hold, extending blessing and lifespan. Fire gives **Wood Communicating with Fire's Brightness**, bringing brilliant literary accomplishment. Metal felling Wood is ominous for people born in Jing or Liang; those from Xu or Yang obtain wealth and distinction. Fire and Earth are welcome, Metal and Water avoided. Luck favors the southeast; the northwest is unfavorable.

Summer Wood is sick at Si, dead at Wu, and entombed at Wei because strong southern Fire drains its true qi. Cloud and rain are auspicious; unbroken clear heat is avoided. In the fourth month, Wood is not yet very weak and Fire not yet very strong. A little Fire gives abundant branches and leaves, shelter, and unfolding blessing. Abundant Water sets the Water spirit adrift: men and women elope in illicit passion. Earth brings profit and reputation. Fire and Earth traveling together, with Ren, Gui, Hai, or Zi in stem and branch, give wealth and distinction. Metal's control inevitably brings calamity or litigation, though for people from Xu or Yang the result is favorable instead.

Wood of the fifth and sixth months welcomes rain and Water; night birth is especially fine. Those obtaining this have wealth, distinction, and longevity. Abundant Fire without Water brings poverty and early death. Summer commands with fierce heat: Fire shatters stone and melts Metal; Wood is in danger of withering. People from Xu or Yang with much stem Fire face wind illness, consumption, and burning calamity. Water's restraint, night birth, or cloudy and rainy weather can turn ill into good. Earth cultivates the root; Water extends the branches. Water and Earth together give not merely wealth and distinction but longevity, health, and peace. Metal cannot control Wood here: Fire is strong, Metal soft, and the child avenges its mother. Yet after Lesser Heat, much Earth is also avoided, since weak Wood cannot control flourishing Earth. For people born in Yan or Qing, it functions as Wealth. In general, summer Wood welcomes Water, unlike Wood at other times.[^26]

During autumn's three months, Wood wants only a fitting measure of clear weather and rain. Prolonged drought withers everything; incessant rain prevents things from ripening. At autumn's beginning, fierce heat has not retreated, so traveling with Fire is unsuitable. Cloudy or rainy birth is best. Metal may seem to threaten cutting and carving, yet Fire qi remains hot and Metal qi has not reached full strength: no harm results. After End of Heat, those born in Jing or Liang avoid Metal. Metal and Water together turn ill into good. Earth cultivating Wood brings profit and reputation. Abundant Water without Earth leaves those from Ji or Yong wandering without a home; for Xu or Yang people, ill turns into good instead.

Wood of the eighth and ninth months is just entering decline and leaf-fall. Daytime birth with Fire traveling alongside brings poverty and early death: autumn sunlight is dry and fierce, and Wood withers and falls. Only night birth, cloud and rain, or Water's relief makes it auspicious. People from Yan or Qing gain literary accomplishment, wealth, and distinction. Yet Water can also set Wood adrift, since autumn Water is not in the season for nourishing Wood; Ji and Yong people especially avoid it. Earth's cultivation makes the foundation steady and substantial. Metal is favorable instead. With Metal in command, the myriad things are being cut back. Wood no longer has growth to fulfill its life, yet has not returned to the root to restore its allotted existence. Metal alone can be borrowed for use. Without Metal's working to make it useful, it is only decayed timber between Heaven and Earth. Hence the axe and adze hew and shape it into beams and pillars. As the saying has it, “Let axes enter the forests in their proper season, and timber will be inexhaustible.” Luck should go east or south.[^27]

Winter Wood stands in the season of stern cutting-back, restoring its allotted existence and returning to the root. A faint impulse of Little Spring remains, showing that creative transformation is never exhausted. The *Changes* says, “The great fruit is not eaten”: this is the meaning. Birth in clear weather is best; cloud and rain make freezing ice and accumulated snow.

Tenth-month Wood meeting Fire is noble and long-lived: ice is cold and Earth frozen, so Fire is needed to warm the roots. Fire and Earth supporting the course give both wealth and distinction. Metal may do no direct harm, yet close kin cannot avoid separation like Shen and Shang. Water offers nourishment and assistance: for Xu and Yang people, profit and reputation follow; for Ji and Yong people, poverty, cold, solitude, and injury to kin.

Wood of Zi and Chou welcomes Fire to thaw it. With Earth alongside, it rises to the highest councils. Water is ominous and injurious. Winter Water commands, and Wood is cold: encountering Water without Fire's warmth freezes it solid and breaks its living impulse. The poor and lowly live long; the wealthy and eminent die young. Metal, though cutting, does no harm: winter Wood returning to its roots gives axe and adze no purchase, while timber already shed and fallen can be hewn into useful vessels. Thus Metal need not bring blame. Those born in Xu, Yang, Yan, or Qing may fall and die prematurely. For Jing, Liang, or Yu people, even the feared injury reaches no further than branches and leaves: the root remains as it was. Ding and Bing in stem and branch providing restraint can bring wealth and distinction, not merely avert calamity. Luck should go south: winter Wood welcomes a southern journey. East comes next.[^27]

#### On Fire 論火

Spring Fire's qi is warm and only beginning to appear. Clear weather borrows Wood to make it bright; rain wets Wood and makes it dim. In the first month, slight yang Fire is hidden within Wood. Though approachable and lovable, it has not escaped the remaining frost and ice. Wood generating it allows yang qi to unfold. Metal is Wealth: Xu and Yang people become rich and devoted to propriety. Earth steals and drains the slight yang, making frivolity, poverty, and low standing.

For Yan, Qing, Xu, and Yang people, how could wealth and distinction be doubted? Earth and Metal are judged as in the first month, except that Earth's strength is somewhat greater in Chen.

#### On Earth 論土

When Earth meets spring, its rich substance and veins begin to stir, all things hold life within, and Wood qi unfolds. The earlier philosophers had good reason to speak of Sickness at Yin, Death at Mao, and Tomb at Chen. Rain condenses yin and wets Earth, leaving the first shoots unable to spread. Clear weather releases the frost and harmonizes yang, allowing life to unfold. Thus spring Earth, as soon as it receives yang qi, can rear the myriad things.

First-month Earth still holds frost and cold: rain freezes it, Water makes ice, and Wood brings Sickness. Only Fire's warmth gives incomparable splendor. Metal restraining Wood also brings reputation and profit. In the second month, Wood is flourishing and Earth collapsing. Wood alongside brings disorders of spleen and stomach, intestinal wind, and hemorrhoidal leakage: illness if light, early death if heavy. Xu and Yang people with Fire in stem and branch and birth in clear daytime suffer no harm. Fire alongside raises one to the highest councils. Water is ominous: for Ji, Yong, Qing, and Yan people, Earth muddied and Water turbid ultimately risk surging Water and collapsing Earth, because Water generates flourishing Wood, which injures Earth. This brings poverty, illness, and early death. Xu, Yang, and Yu people with Fire in stem and branch find it auspicious instead. Metal draining Earth's qi makes calamity difficult to avoid.[^29]

Wood brings illness or early death, though people from Xu, Yang, and Yu are unharmed. Water and Wood at the same station bring poverty and distress, particularly for Ji, Yong, Yan, and Qing people. Metal restraining Wood turns ill into good. Luck welcomes the south; the west comes next.

Wood brings illness and early death without doubt. Water as Wealth makes Xu and Yang people affluent. Metal together with Wood brings distinction; together with Water, poverty.

#### On Metal 論金

In spring, Wood flourishes and Fire is supported: this is not the time when Metal obtains qi. Its Extinction at Yin, Embryo at Mao, and Nurturing at Chen express the inexhaustibility of creative transformation. Clear weather is therefore auspicious for spring Metal; cloud and rain bring obstruction. Earth generating it is precisely what is needed: at the approach to extinction, an impulse of life remains.

First-month Metal sees Wood as Wealth, but Wood is too strong and gives only enough for clothing and sustenance. Earth qi is still cold and cannot yet generate and assist: one gains a name in a skill or art, nothing more. Fire alongside makes men and women marry repeatedly. Fire with Earth brings exceptional wealth and distinction. Water's drainage makes solitary poverty and weakness.

In the second and third months, Earth gives Metal an inexhaustible impulse of generation, indicating wealth, distinction, and longevity. Water drains its originating qi and likewise brings poverty and lack of feeling. Fire makes confined Metal encounter a ghost: poverty and early death without doubt. Earth's generation or Water's restraint is auspicious. Wood brings hardship and obstruction. Spring Wood is strong, and feeble Metal trying to control it is like an infant resisting a powerful enemy: plainly it cannot withstand it. This must bring the danger of seeking glory and obtaining humiliation instead, together with litigation and contention. Humanity and righteousness punish one another: that is the meaning. For Jing, Liang, and Yu people, the encounter brings wealth; Xu and Yang people with abundant Earth in stem and branch gain distinction. Luck in Earth territory is best, Metal territory next.[^30]

Fire blazes and melts Metal: poverty and early death predominate, or wealth with early death, lust, and low standing. Wood as Wealth often brings riches and distinction to Jing, Liang, and Yu people. #### On Water 論水

Who would not call spring Water weakest of all, sick at Yin, dead at Mao, and entombed at Chen? Yet they do not recognize that Water is yin qi, born at Shen and flourishing at Zi. In autumn and winter its qi gathers without dispersing, so Water commonly dries up. When spring comes, yang qi steams upward and yin qi descends; rain and dew moisten the world and Water begins to grow. This is the ordinary course of Water's force. The astrological specialists have good reason to say that living Water is born at Mao. Clear weather makes spring Water gently swelling; rain makes it boundless and overflowing.[^31]

Those born in the first month have cold qi in their Water. Fire melts ice and releases frost, bringing wealth, distinction, and ease. Metal's assistance is best for those born in Xu or Yang. Wood without Fire leaves Water cold and Wood chilled, with no living impulse. Earth controlling Water also brings poverty and cold. Earth's restraint together with Metal's generation gives plentiful clothing and sustenance.

Water of the second and third months extends without limit. Earth provides embankments: by day, wealth and distinction; by night, wandering and displacement. Birth after Grain Rain may bring sexual misconduct or disorders of numbness and debility, since Earth is muddied and Water turbid. Fire makes Water and Fire injure one another: calamity and lawsuits are unavoidable. Metal generating Water causes unfeeling overflow, though Xu and Yang people with Earth in stem and branch are unharmed. Wood draining it enables generosity toward others. Yan and Qing people born in the middle ten days of the second month meet Wood at full strength: it steals their originating spirit and produces wind illness and weakness. Metal's assistance averts the trouble.

If Earth's spirit is seen upon Yin or Si, it is Gui's Officer, and Hai or Zi is again unnecessary. Their appearance makes Earth contend for mastery and creates mixture.〉[^108]

〈Gui takes Wu as Officer. Ji encountered puts yin and yang out of order and becomes a ghost instead. If Jia Wood's qi brings release and rescue, obstruction and discord may still be avoided. If Jichou or Jiwei and the like attack from both sides, Gui's blessing becomes scarcely visible.〉[^108]

## Translator’s endnotes

[^01]: **Text and witnesses.** **S** is the repository’s [Chinese juan four](../source/juan-04.md), exported from [Wikisource revision 657371](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=657371); its checksum is recorded in the file header. **G602** and **G603** are parallel passages in the *Gujin Tushu Jicheng*, Arts volumes [602, revision 1942565](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=1942565), and [603, revision 1942566](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=1942566). **U** is the shorter, unlabelled [Wikisource juan four, revision 2119828](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=2119828). These are consulted transcriptions, not a claim to independent line-by-line collation of historical facsimiles. S controls the selection and sequence; alternative readings are identified where used. Agreement among online texts does not establish independent descent. The separately transmitted Siku editorial preface is not translated here. English section numbering is editorial.

[^02]: **Changing analytical frames.** The opening discussions generally take the day stem as their reference. Later passages also use the birth-year stem, branch, nayin, and the “true” or transformed Five Phases. A Wood person in one passage is therefore not automatically a Wood Day Master in another. “Ghost” translates 鬼 when an overcoming influence is discussed as hostile or ungoverned; it can become useful Officer through appropriate restraint. “Body,” “root,” “ruler,” and “useful influence” are retained according to their local argument, not flattened into one modern formula. The source’s claims about health, lifespan, gender, morality, and rank are translated as historical claims, not established facts about living people. Common graphic confusions among 己, 已, and 巳 are normalized where grammar and the stated stem or branch determine the reading.

[^03]: **Opening Wood images.** “Little Spring” 小春 names the early-winter season, not a second literal spring. The text distinguishes structural timber from living vegetation, then changes to seasonal and relational arguments; these images are not exhaustive botanical definitions of Jia and Yi. Fire may first injure Officer and subsequently generate Wealth. The reversal is explicit and essential: the unfavorable opening rule is not meant to stand independently of the following rescue.

[^04]: **The six-day poems and a missing Yi line.** Each stem occurs with six branches in the sixty-pair cycle. S’s Yi poem, however, supplies only five branch lines, omitting You. G602 includes, between Hai and Chou: 酉中辛剋恐遭傷, “Within You, Xin’s control threatens injury,” with the explanation that Yi is extinct at You, Xin is Seven Killings, transformation makes it auspicious, its absence ominous, and a Xinsi hour brings distinction through transformed qi and Metal Spirit. That parallel is translated here in the note, not silently imported into S’s body. In the annotated poems, bold sentences are the verse and angled brackets the source commentary. The named Messenger 傳送 is Shen; Great Impulse 太衝 is Mao; Lesser Auspiciousness 小吉 is Wei. These are traditional branch titles, not additional planets.

[^05]: **Officer, Killings, and proportional restraint.** The repeated paragraphs are retained rather than replaced by one summary. The text distinguishes mixed Officer/Killings, unrestrained Killings, and Killings rendered usable through restraint. “Removal and retention” 去留 concerns selecting or clearing competing influences; it does not mean deleting a pillar from the birth record. “Depth” within a solar term concerns the relative development of seasonal qi. The source itself requires strength, support, and timing before judging a named influence.

[^06]: **Bing and Ding are not interchangeable fire images.** Bing is solar qi, Ding working fire. Their initial favorable directions differ from later prescriptions under other conditions. The verse’s “Even wealth and distinction are not wholly secure” retains 不週全, also found in G602, despite the neighboring recommendation of western and northern courses. It has not been changed to an uncomplicated promise of success merely to harmonize the compilation.

[^07]: **Jiji, Water and Fire in Completion.** 既濟 is the *Changes’* image of having crossed successfully, rendered “Already Across” in juan two’s trigram discussion. Here it describes Water and Fire brought into a working balance. It is not simply the presence of both phases: the paragraph excludes arrangements in which either extinguishes or dries up the other, and insists on support from month and hour.

[^08]: **A page classification interrupts Bing’s verse.** S inserts the Siku library classification between 戌傍 and 財鄉, splitting a single verse line. The translation joins the line and does not treat the catalogue label as an utterance of the poem. The classification is represented at the end of the juan. Statements such as Xin’s Birth within Zi belong to the reverse yin-stage scheme, not to a newly added hidden-stem assignment.

[^09]: **Earth’s two kinds of work.** Walls need integrity; cultivated soil needs opening. This contrast explains the opposite initial judgments concerning punishment and clash. Gouchen 勾陳 is retained as a traditional configuration name rather than reduced to an ordinary physical hook. The text’s statement that Water “controls” Earth is kept as its language of pressure and restraint; it is not used to replace the ordinary Five-Phase control cycle. Favorable directions change with the configuration under discussion.

[^10]: **Kuigang exceptions.** The special Kuigang rules are explicitly separated from the ordinary Wealth/Officer rules for the other days. Wuxu, Gengchen, Gengxu, and Renchen receive such treatment below. Where a day instead participates in a different configuration, the text permits a different judgment. “Repetition” here must be read within those conditions, not as a universal recommendation to accumulate identical pillars.

[^11]: **Metal imagery and a retained directional variant.** The lists of metals, quicksilver, cinnabar, and jewels are the source’s classificatory images, not a modern chemical definition of the phase Metal. The initial rejection of Ding for Xin is followed by a conditional case favoring exposed Bing and Ding; both are retained. S’s Gengxu commentary favors Water luck, whereas G602 has Wood luck. S is retained: the comparison does not justify making all the Metal passages prescribe the same direction. The short verse likewise says “east and west,” not a silently substituted southeast.

[^12]: **Wu, not Xu.** In Xin’s Si-day commentary S reads 丙戌建祿在巳. G602 has 丙戊建祿在巳, “Bing and Wu establish Lu at Si,” which fits the Officer/Seal pair explicitly under discussion. The translation adopts Wu in brackets. “Inverted Food” 倒食 is the source’s name for the obstructive resource influence; it is not literally food eaten upside down.

[^13]: **Competing Water images and conditional directions.** S first calls Gui boundless seawater, then explicitly reports the alternative rain-and-dew image. Neither is suppressed to agree with a familiar modern mnemonic. “Dark Warrior” translates 玄武, the northern emblem used as a configuration title. The opening preference for Fire–Earth luck and later preference for Metal–Water assistance belong to different strength conditions in the passage; they have not been consolidated into one unconditional rule.

[^14]: **Invisible invocation is not an ordinary visible clash.** The Renzi and Guihai accounts invoke absent Wealth/Officer positions through the named Flying-Heaven method. The text itself excludes special Punishment and Arched Combination configurations from the preceding general rule. Renchen’s “Lu established” in the verse is retained although the preceding juan locates Ren’s ordinary Lu at Hai. Here Lu also participates in the language of official emolument and success.

[^15]: **The Zi-month distinction and an elliptical warning.** The source first groups Bing and Ding under harmonious Officer, then explicitly singles out Ding as Indirect Officer through two yins. The second statement qualifies the first; the translation does not erase it. Recurrent 歲運傷為福之地 is elliptical. In its repeated setting after 忌, it is read as a warning against year or course injuring the position that supplies blessing, not as “injury itself creates blessing.” G602 has the same compressed wording, so this is a contextual interpretation rather than an attested character correction.

[^16]: **Wealth at Birth and unseen forms.** 長生財 is not automatically Wealth already manifest in the birth-month branch. The passage repeatedly requires exposed Wealth before allowing the name; otherwise it diagnoses an output-dominant month and seeks another configuration. 不見之形 refers to invoked or implied positions, as in the arching methods already discussed, not to invisible celestial bodies. A phrase such as 庚時申時 is rendered as an hour’s Geng stem or Shen branch, not the single pillar Gengshen. Explicitly named stem–branch pillars remain written as one name.

[^17]: **Gradual entry into monthly command.** S’s 交卯末是卯 and 交辰末是辰 are read with G602’s 未, “not yet,” consistent with the Chou introduction. The annotation distinguishes the first residual qi from the later ruling qi; it is not saying the calendrical branch has not changed at all. The seven-day, seven-and-a-half-day, and subsequent eight-day allocations are retained where they differ. They should not be converted into an exact modern schedule without settling the source’s calendrical conventions. Several remarks extend the same branch interpretation to days and hours under different seasons.

[^18]: **Opening a Storehouse is conditional.** The recurrent rule is: if the needed influence is hidden, a clash can bring it forth; after emergence, that same clash can injure it. It is not a universal endorsement of clashing every Storehouse. 雜氣 is Mixed Qi: the term identifies the season-ending branches’ combination of residual and ruling influences, not confusion or textual corruption.

[^19]: **Earthly corners and heavenly monthly command.** The distinction between Qian/Kun/Gen/Xun as directional corners and month qi is the compiler’s own. It also recalls juan two’s dispute about assigning independent days of command to subsidiary influences. The translation does not harmonize these discussions by inventing a single schedule acceptable to every authority quoted.

[^20]: **Bing and Ding, not a Bingzi day.** S has 丙子生巳月 where the discussion concerns the two Fire stems. G602 reads 丙丁生巳月. The bracketed correction restores the parallel with the surrounding paired-stem entries. It does not alter any genuinely specified Bingzi example.

[^21]: **Why the Gengshen exception matters.** The source does not leave “unrestrained Killings brings early death” unqualified: it immediately exempts a case with both generation and a rooted body. Its final four contrasts inspect which exposed stem suppresses another influence. These are separate conditions, not an instruction to require all four pairs simultaneously.

[^22]: **Wei’s residual period.** The paragraph says “after seven days” and then specifies “seven and a half days.” Both figures occur in S and G602. The translation retains the discrepancy rather than assigning a falsely exact boundary. Lu and Horse Sharing One Region here belongs to the Wealth/Officer vocabulary, not necessarily a simultaneous ordinary Lu-position and Traveling-Horse lookup.

[^23]: **Exposed through emergence or clash.** 官透衝 and 印透衝 are compressed expressions. The translation reads them in light of the section’s repeatedly stated distinction between an influence already emerging and a hidden influence brought out by clash. It does not turn them into a prescription to clash an already exposed Officer or Seal in every case.

[^24]: **Too many combinations.** The Hai-month Ding sentence places 多合 after the aversions. It is translated as excessive combination being avoided, unlike earlier 官愛多合, “Officer likes many combinations.” The difference is left visible. The source’s conditions, and the distinction between combination that secures an influence and combination that entangles or removes it, prevent an unrestricted rule that more combination is always better.

[^25]: **Place and weather as additional conditions.** Ji 冀, Qing 青, Yan 兗, Xu 徐, Yang 揚, Jing 荊, Liang 梁, Yong 雍, and Yu 豫 are the traditional Nine Provinces, not nine modern administrative provinces with identical boundaries. The source argues that the Eight Characters alone do not exhaust differences in endowment; it introduces location, daylight, and weather to explain them. This is its explanatory claim, not an independently tested meteorological model. Its opening assignment of both autumn and winter as Metal’s favorable time is retained.

[^26]: **Living Wood, seasonal danger, and apparent contradictions.** The images of blooming, uprooting, parching, and cultivation shift with season and locality. Hence Water can be beneficial in summer but chilling, excessive, or uprooting elsewhere. The second-month sentence simultaneously predicts early death from the Earth–Metal–Water meeting and allows longevity under poverty. Both clauses are retained, not made into a single unqualified prognosis. Historical names for wind illness, consumption, and related disorders are not assigned precise modern diagnoses.

[^27]: **Axe in season and uneaten fruit.** The axe-and-forest passage invokes the classical ideal of taking timber only at the appropriate season; the “great fruit not eaten” is the *Changes’* image of a surviving seed of renewal. The moral and literary analogies are preserved alongside the technical argument. S’s compressed 有隕自夭 is rendered as falling and premature death without specifying an otherwise unattested accident. The advice to travel east or south concerns the directional phase classification of luck, not necessarily physical migration.

[^28]: **Sunlight, Fire, and the Mother Luminary.** The source can praise warmth while warning that Fire contends with the sun. These claims occur within its day/night and regional distinctions and are not harmonized into a modern temperature rule. “Mother” is the generating phase: Metal’s child Water may retaliate against Fire; Wood then mediates the sequence. “Luminary” 曜 elsewhere can mark an astrological significator, not only a visible planet.

[^29]: **Long Summer and traditional illness language.** 長夏 is the Earth-associated late-summer interval. The imagery of cracked fields and loosened soil explains the passage’s preference for moisture and moderation. Terms concerning spleen, stomach, intestinal wind, leakage, phlegm, or wind and blood retain their premodern scope. The translation neither recasts them as diagnoses nor derives treatment advice from the phase relationships.

[^30]: **Metal’s working and its buried light.** The Golden Horse and Jade Hall, jade belts, and golden fish are images of literary or official distinction. The statement that Earth obscures autumn Metal is followed by the quotation that buried Metal becomes stronger; the difference between strength and manifest usefulness is not erased. The winter discussion likewise first praises Fire’s warmth and then warns against it in an early-winter case. These are transmitted conditional judgments, not a corrected unified doctrine.

[^31]: **“Living Water” and different models of Birth.** The assertion that living Water is born at Mao differs from the ordinary phase cycle beginning at Shen and from individual yin-stem stages. The source explicitly introduces it as an astrological account of spring circulation; it is not silently substituted for every Birth position. “Commanding star” 令星 in the eighth/ninth-month clause remains as transmitted rather than being assigned an unprinted planet or stem. The final Earth-and-Metal phrase is likewise retained without inventing a conjunction absent from S.

[^32]: **The opening verse-and-commentary disagreements.** Sections 4–8 retain each verse followed by its commentary. The first Jia/Yi commentary praises exposed Bing and then says Ding is not feared but Bing is; G603 shares this difficulty. The sentence is retained, not silently inverted. “Removal or pairing” 去配 covers dealing with an interfering influence by removal or an appropriate combination. Crooked-and-Straight 曲直, Kindred Images 類象, and Hastening toward Qian 趨乾 are named configurations, not ordinary verbs detached from the method.

[^33]: **Wealth and distinction westward.** S’s 行西富背 is read as 行西富貴, supported by G603. The four examples are preserved as four separate four-pillar charts in their transmitted order. Their purpose is to contrast choosing Metal–Water with choosing Fire–Earth, not to recommend mixing both prescriptions indiscriminately.

[^34]: **One continuous autumn explanation, and a repaired pillar.** S breaks the long small-type commentary between 丙丁 and 戊己; these are one continuous exclusion, “without Bing, Ding, Wu, or Ji.” The translation joins it. S’s 甲戊 in the second chart is not a possible stem–branch pillar; G603 gives 甲戌, Jiaxu, adopted in brackets. G603’s separate variant 戊丙 in the preceding “Wu alone used” clause is not imported: S’s statement is retained. The offices and examination rank are historical status descriptions, not independent authentication of the example biographies.

[^35]: **Winter Seal, and an example outside the stated season.** The first commentary’s 食傷兩旺, Food and Hurting both strong, is retained against G603’s 身傷, body and Hurting. The following late-spring/summer discussion nevertheless cites a Dingyou-month chart. That seasonal mismatch belongs to S and the parallel; the example is not silently changed to a spring month. The central claim is that a favorable-sounding Lu direction may be unfavorable to the actual influences being used.

[^36]: **Killings grouped as one system.** In the Jiashen/You-month case, the source explicitly treats abundant Metal together as Killings instead of mechanically declaring every Geng/Xin appearance a disqualifying mixture. This is its local interpretive exception, not a universal cancellation of the earlier mixed-Officer warning. The preceding childlessness examples and the phrase about Wood’s succession are preserved as attributed judgments, not modern reproductive claims.

[^37]: **Combination can remove needed restraint.** Slight Geng combining with Yi can deprive a strong Jia of the regulating influence it needed. Thus a named combination is not automatically beneficial. In the repeated-Jiaxu commentary, S’s 上火多 is read as 土火多, “much Earth and Fire,” supported by G603. The repair is bracketed; it does not change the separately stated preference for one suitably placed Fire or Metal.

[^38]: **Yisi versus Yichou/Jisi, and two directional lists.** The verse says 乙巳時, Yisi hour, while its commentary gives 乙丑己巳, Yichou or Jisi. G603 repeats the mismatch. A Jia day ordinarily derives Jisi, not Yisi, at Si; the verse is therefore suspect, but remains visible as transmitted. The next verse rejects Water–Wood, while its commentary rejects Metal–Water; both are retained. The distinction between an ordinary useful influence and a Metal-Spirit configuration is the commentary’s own qualification.

[^39]: **Obstruction changing to Peace, and the bear dream.** S’s 反秦 is read as 反泰, “turns to Peace,” with G603 and the explicit contrast with 否, Obstruction. The bear dream 夢叶羆 is retained as an auspicious classical allusion rather than reduced to a generic success claim; such imagery can evoke the arrival of a worthy figure as well as the more familiar auspicious birth associations. The text does not provide a separate worked prediction of a child’s sex.

[^40]: **Few versus many is relative to season and support.** The verse and examples distinguish spring Jia from autumn Jia, and spring Yi from autumn Yi; Seal changes the outcome. The charts are not modernized to make every stem–branch relationship satisfy a single simplified rule. The first Gengshen/three-Metal example is explicitly deficient in Seal, which is the contrast the commentary draws.

[^41]: **Changing use midway is not automatically improvement.** The western-luck discussion warns against abandoning a functioning Fire–Earth arrangement for Metal that then battles the original Fire. “Arriving without arriving, departing without departing” preserves that rhetorical impasse. The next spring-Yi discussion includes a Si-month example; its change of season is retained rather than altered to fit the opening title.

[^42]: **Hour formulas and competing strength qualifications.** The Gengchen/Xinsi verses and their commentary discuss rooted Metal and the removal of interfering Fire, not the mere presence of two auspicious-looking hour names. The later northward exception explicitly requires both body and useful influence to be strong. The text thus qualifies its own general preference for the south when Fire is used.

[^43]: **Two unresolved readings in summer Yi.** The verse says 乙丙若無, “if Yi and Bing are absent,” although Yi is the day stem and the commentary objects to Bing and Ding. G603 repeats it; the translation preserves it rather than silently replacing Yi with Ding. The commentary then says 己丑日, a Jichou day, within an Yi-day discussion. G603 again agrees. An Yichou reading, or a lost division involving Si and Chou, is plausible, but no secure repair is established here. The conspicuous Jichou remains visible.

[^44]: **Killings can be preferred to Officer.** The autumn-Yi paragraph expressly prefers a usable Killings arrangement to a lone, unsupported Geng Officer. It then distinguishes helpful restraint from repeated Ding producing excessive restraint. The compiler’s statement about officials “today” refers to his own historical setting, not a survey of modern officeholders.

[^45]: **Conflicting Yi/Metal verses and the seventy-three-year example.** One quotation favors adding the Si–You–Chou class with Seal; another warns against Si and Chou. Both are retained as transmitted authorities. 死二子 in the final example is rendered as the deaths of two sons, not as the subject’s death at seventy-three. The source does not independently document the case or establish a biological cause.

[^46]: **Leading and following.** 丑引戌從 describes an arrangement around the day and hour; it is not a literal procession. The verse’s compressed 己酉 is understood as Si or You from the commentary’s explicit Yisi and Yiyou contrast. The named officials and comparative rank are reproduced as the source’s examples, without supplying biographies not established by the text.

[^47]: **Companions may carry a heavy use.** The two final Wood discussions distinguish unwanted duplication in already-strong Wood from assistance needed to sustain strong output and Wealth. “Brothers” is the source’s relational image, not necessarily a prediction about actual siblings. The quoted songs retain their differing directional prescriptions. They have not been rewritten to make Water universally beneficial or Companions universally harmful.

[^48]: **Spring mixtures and the unusual name Feijing.** 飛晶 is transmitted here and in G603; “Flying Radiance” retains that wording rather than silently substituting the familiar Flying-Heaven title. It is a named configuration, whose full requirements are not supplied in this paragraph. The grain-abstaining ascetic image is translated, not recommended as a dietary practice. The spring paragraph permits one or two repeated Water stems when their interference is resolved; it is not a simple numerical ban on repetition.

[^49]: **The long autumn Fire commentary.** The verse’s 甲日, “a Jia day,” is retained although the surrounding subject is Bing and Ding; G603 agrees. S’s 四桂 is read as 四柱, Four Pillars, supported by G603. The reading 陽字, “yang characters,” is retained against G603’s 酉字, “You characters,” in the eighth-month Bing clause. In the Dingyou sentence, punctuation permits different groupings of Officer, Companions, and Seal; the translation follows S’s natural grouping that Officer is unfavorable while Companions and Seal are not feared. The contradictory-sounding directional qualifications remain visible.

[^50]: **Unrooted transformation and “Jia Food.”** “Without generation” does not mean stillbirth: it concerns the absence of a generating influence in a following or transforming arrangement. The Bingshen commentary’s 壬煞甲食, “Ren Killings and Jia Food,” is retained though Jia is not Bing’s ordinary Food God. G603 shares the wording. Jia could be described relative to Ren, or the text could be damaged; no universal reassignment of the Ten Gods is inferred. The paragraph explicitly denies that removing already-feeble Ren with strong Wu is necessarily helpful restraint.

[^51]: **Two combinations can protect one another.** The winter-Bing explanation distinguishes Bing–Xin transformation from Ding–Ren combination, the latter occupying Ding so it cannot injure Xin. This does not mean every multiple combination is favorable. “Blazing Upward,” “Inverted Flight,” “Kindred Images,” and “Following Strength” remain named configurations, not interchangeable labels for any chart containing much Fire.

[^52]: **General rules have named-configuration exceptions.** The Bing/Ji paragraph allows Officer under particular Earth/output arrangements, rather than treating every occurrence as “Hurting Officer sees Officer” with one inevitable outcome. In the Ding/Ren verse, Dui names You and Kun names Shen in the accompanying branch warning. “Proper” and “partial” enclosures are the source’s distinction; they are not silently replaced by one modern list of permitted transformation months.

[^53]: **The autumn-Ding examples do not all occur in autumn.** The Gao Wenjian chart has a Guimao month, although the opening verse specifies autumn or winter. The commentary invokes Dingmao’s capacity to bear repeated Water and is retained with its example. It is not changed to an autumn-month pillar. Assertions of office and illness remain the compiler’s illustrative reports, not independently verified biographies.

[^54]: **Ding’s light and a difficult celestial phrase.** 房日之源太陰之餘 is rendered close to the transmitted wording, “the source of Fang’s sun, the remnant of Greater Yin.” 房日 may invoke the solar correspondence of the mansion Fang, but the whole phrase remains uncertain; the comparison transcription does not establish a secure reconstruction. 元地, “original territory,” is also left without an invented branch identification. The stone-and-bamboo image concerns obtaining fire and the paradox of its visibility in darkness, not an instruction to practice a particular ignition technique.

[^55]: **Ding and Ji at Si.** The verse’s 丁巳居蛇 is read with the explicit commentary on 丁巳己巳, Dingsi and Jisi, as Ding and Ji dwelling at Snake. The stated incomplete punishment and its later completion are preserved; the same extra branch can change the configuration rather than merely add another auspicious influence. The two Wushen-hour examples in the preceding paragraph are retained separately.

[^56]: **The final Ding course list is compressed.** S says 丑壬中行, travel through Chou and Ren, combining a branch and stem designation; G603 repeats it. It is not silently regularized to two adjacent branches. The source also distinguishes the length of a favorable interval from the mere appearance of a favorable influence. Several repeated lists vary; the translation preserves those variations rather than creating one synthetic itinerary.

[^57]: **Spring Earth: different uses within one season.** The long commentary distinguishes first-month Wu from Ji, second-month Officer from Killings, and third-month Wealth from a Jiayin-hour configuration. Its conditional reversals are all retained. “Lord of fields and houses” translates the verse’s 田舍郎 in its context of landed wealth, without implying an official title. The statement that Water may make a southern course favorable is not a general preference for flooding Earth.

[^58]: **Two difficult summer-Earth clauses.** The sequence 戊日午月戊寅日甲寅時戊午時 compresses more than one possible example; the translation keeps each named day/month/hour but does not manufacture one four-pillar chart from them. 六月用在七月近秋作六月推 is also compressed: it is preserved as a boundary discussion, not converted into a precise solar-calendar algorithm. The commentary distinguishes using Metal–Water from using Fire–Wood and separately recognizes Arched configurations.

[^59]: **The autumn commentary contains an unresolved blemish description.** 若丙有庚及申露或隠寅無制 appears in both S and G603 and is retained close to its wording; the relation among exposed Bing, Geng, Shen, and concealed Yin is not fully explicit. S’s 巳上七月 is read as 己土七月, “Ji Earth in the seventh month,” supported by G603’s 已土 and the paired Wu/Ji argument. Elsewhere this paragraph’s apparent permission of Officer/Seal while using Metal conflicts with adjacent warnings; no harmonizing deletion has been made.

[^60]: **Four configurations and two historical exemplars.** Ban Chao and Jilun are invoked as images of high rank and immense wealth, not as authenticated natal cases in this passage. The fourfold list distinguishes forming Water, following Wealth, Combining Lu, and transformation into Fire. Its use of “transformation” is broader than simply seeing a familiar pair of stems. S’s concluding favorable Si is retained against G603’s Zi in the Chou-month course list.

[^61]: **“Following the Killings configuration” need not be technical self-abandonment.** In the Jiayin-hour verse, 從煞格 is followed by a commentary permitting restraint when seasonally appropriate. It is therefore rendered as following the Killings configuration’s method, not automatically as the special weak-body Abandoning-the-Self configuration described later. The preceding Wu transformation paragraph itself mentions both Ren and Gui; the translation does not quietly remove Ren to enforce one modern combination rule.

[^62]: **Exposed Metal, rooted Bing, and the alternative Wood use.** The compressed Owl/Bing/Shen sentence is retained without replacing a stem merely to make the warning systematic. The passage subsequently switches to an alternative Wood-based use, and that switch is explicit in English. “The hour’s ruling place” translates 時主之地 cautiously; it is not changed into an unprinted branch or numerical age.

[^63]: **Strong body with Killings versus weak following.** The Chen/Xu example welcomes Killings because the body is not weak and the two can harmonize. Restraining away that useful Killings can therefore be unfavorable. This differs from the later Jimao/Jiwei example, where a weak body abandons itself to the prevailing influence. The two should not be conflated into a blanket rule against restraint.

[^64]: **Ji, Si, and already.** In these paired-stem discussions the source often prints 巳 where the context requires 己, Ji. In 丁巳重見 the translation reads repeated Ding and Ji as the strengthening influences incompatible with the stated following arrangement, rather than silently introducing a complete Dingsi pillar. In 庚辛巳露 below, 巳 is read as 已, “already,” not as an additional Snake branch. These are contextual graphic normalizations, not a new system of stem assignments.

[^65]: **Chou/Hai supported; another verse retained.** S’s 丑寅時 in the weak-Ji verse is read as 丑亥時, Chou or Hai hour, following G603. Yi is derived at those hours for a Ji day, fitting the commentary’s hourly Killings. The later verse genuinely says 戌子, Xu or Zi, in S; G603’s 戊子 is not substituted. The four following charts are all retained, including the distinction between rank, wealth, and rank obtained by contribution.

[^66]: **Food before, Killings after.** 食前煞後 is a positional qualification, not a claim that Food God and Killings are the same influence. The final Gouchen stanza makes the central distinction once more: Fire can protect the useful Wood from Metal, yet excessive Fire destroys both Wood’s usefulness and Earth’s condition. Its title is preserved as a technical name, not offered as a universally favorable label.

[^67]: **The Gengchen month correction.** S repeats “second month” before the Gengchen day/hour/month discussion. G603 gives 三月, third month, adopted in brackets; Chen is the third monthly branch in the preceding table. The rest of the spring paragraph retains its several alternative uses rather than making a rootless exposed Officer sufficient for distinction. “Both courses” preserves 兩行 where the source does not spell out the two directions again.

[^68]: **Officer and Killings have different Water aversions.** The summer-Metal paragraph explicitly distinguishes using Bing as Xin’s Officer from using Ding as Killings. Ren may attack the former while combining with the latter. The translation therefore retains the apparently opposing Water prescriptions. The broad statement that Chou, You, and Hai are where Officer/Killings enter Tomb is left as the source’s compressed language, not converted into a standardized twelve-stage table.

[^69]: **Autumn Metal is not simply “the stronger, the better.”** Geng requires suitable control and support; Xin’s cases distinguish one Bing from several, and repeated Ding from a single useful Killings. The same Fire course can therefore regulate one arrangement and overdrive another. This is the local argument, not a statistical claim about the number of Fire stems in successful people’s charts.

[^70]: **Flying Lu and Flanking the Mound.** 飛祿 and 夾丘 are retained as separate named configurations. Their instructions concerning absent or invoked Officer positions cannot be replaced by the ordinary rule that winter Metal welcomes Fire. The text itself exempts those configurations from an otherwise favorable Officer/Killings course. No missing full construction method for 夾丘 is invented here.

[^71]: **A supported negation and an ambiguous group label.** S’s 乃逢戊己, “then encountering Wu and Ji,” conflicts with the Water-use argument. G603 reads 不逢戊己, “without encountering Wu and Ji,” which is adopted in brackets. The list ending in Jichou and Jihai and the statement “all have Companions” remain compressed; Ji itself is not Metal’s Companion, so this is not treated as a new Ten-Gods assignment. In the next stanza, 丁巳 is read as Ding and Ji, matching the explicit Officer/Seal pairing, not as a single Dingsi pillar.

[^72]: **Urgency in Flanking Killings.** 夾煞 names a configuration whose strong impetus may yield either striking success or severe harm when activated. The brambles in the verse are a literary image of downfall, not an independently specified manner of death. The subsequent warning concerns rousing the existing conflict through the luck course.

[^73]: **Do not force every Water configuration into Well-Railing Cross.** The commentary says that incomplete cases are judged as Hurting Officer, and a visible Bing may require a Killings judgment instead. In the later Water-guiding paragraph, a Gengchen hour is listed and then Chen hour is warned against; G603 repeats the difficulty. The sequence is retained rather than silently removing one occurrence. “The child generates Wealth” uses generation relationships, not a literal claim that the person’s child supplies money.

[^74]: **Verse and commentary name different stems.** The Xinwei/Xinmao verse welcomes Ding and Bing, while its commentary says Ding and Yi. Both are preserved, as in G603. The later Si/Xu explanation retains S’s Ji and Wu as the influences breaking the offending Water; G603 has a different, also awkward 巳戌 reading. No unsupported reconciliation is imposed. Tao Zhu is a classical exemplar of wealth, not a natal chart supplied here.

[^75]: **All-yin and changing expectations.** The all-yin paragraph is not dismissed solely for lacking Fire; the text makes configuration and use decisive. S’s 五陰之干, “the five yin stems,” is retained against G603’s 正陰之干. This is why the following generalizations about weak Xin must be read with their qualifications rather than as an absolute preference for weakness.

[^76]: **Southern luck both feared and permitted.** The verse calls the south ominous in an autumn/winter Officer–Killings case, while the commentary permits it when Fire and Wood constitute a fitting use. The distinction is retained. 歸致之鄉 in the Bing–Jia–Ren commentary remains obscure and is translated as the region of return and convergence; it is not silently emended to a modern favorable-direction instruction.

[^77]: **Metal–Water Hurting Officer is a qualified formula.** The commentary expressly confines the familiar approval of Officer to appropriate autumn/winter conditions and rejects extending it automatically to spring or summer. The last warning’s list of exhaustion, Extinction, and Tomb is retained as a compressed description, not represented as a newly verified stage assignment for every named branch.

[^78]: **Wind and Cloud, and spring-Water exceptions.** Yin/Tiger and Chen/Dragon underlie the Wind-and-Cloud image; the text later makes those branch correspondences explicit. The early first-/second-month formula and the Punishment-Combination rules have different requirements, so their aversions are not consolidated. S’s 無根上多 is read with G603’s 無根土多, “rootless [Wood], with much Earth.” The sentence is kept within the Jia-Wood warning rather than made a universal rule that an unrooted day ruler benefits from more Earth.

[^79]: **Three named hours called two.** The sixth-month passage lists Bingchen, Dingsi, and Xinsi but calls them “two hours”; G603 repeats the inconsistency. All three names and the inconsistent count are preserved. The Renshen exception also retains the source’s unusual claim that exposed Bing and Jia can form Killings–Seal; it is not silently substituted with a more familiar stem pair. “Hastening toward Gen” is a named configuration, not physical travel northeast.

[^80]: **A seasonal contradiction and a course variant.** The seventh-month Ren sentence says that both year and month are Yin, although the seventh monthly branch is Shen. G603 repeats 歲月俱寅; the translation leaves the contradiction visible rather than conjecturally changing “month” to “day.” S’s Xu-and-Yin course in the later Gui clause is retained against G603’s Wuyin. The interrupted source commentary at 比／肩 is joined into “Companions,” without omitting either part.

[^81]: **Invoking Si versus having Earth already present.** 飛祿, 合祿, and 邀巳 depend on different arrangements; once substantial Officer or Killings is already present, the source directs a different analysis rather than invoking a missing position mechanically. The broken commentary across 遇／印 is joined. 飛玷 is retained as blemishes to the Flying configuration against G603’s 飛祿. The winter-Gui sentence concerning no Seal and exposed Earth/Metal is grammatically compressed; the translation reads the negation with Seal/Earth/Metal in the stated Jia–Yin arrangement, not as a new universally applicable rule.

[^82]: **Late flowering is not lasting wealth.** The peach-blossom-after-rain verse distinguishes a brief improvement from a durable source of fortune. The ensuing seventh-month Ren commentary introduces Owl and Food in opposition; it is retained without assuming that every Seal is harmful or that every further generating influence improves the chart.

[^83]: **Jia useless in the verse, useful in the commentary.** The Ren/Water-configuration stanza says 甲無功, “Jia has no achievement,” but its commentary allows Yin/Jia Food to guide the strong Water and develop southeastward. G603 shares the contrast, which is retained. “Six animal branches” means the six branch partners possible for Ren in the sexagenary cycle, not six additional animal spirits. 散月 is rendered broadly as various months; no unprinted restricted month list is supplied.

[^84]: **Geng and Wu versus Gengxu.** In Riding the Dragon’s Back, S distinguishes the stems Geng and Wu. G603 sometimes writes Gengxu, an apparent conflation; S’s repeated wording and its Wuchen-year example govern the translation. The second chart contains Geng yet is still called noble for its Jia and complete Yin/Chen structure. This qualification is preserved rather than deleting a stem to make the examples mechanically uniform.

[^85]: **An extra Bingchen after the chart.** The sequence 癸巳己未壬午庚子丙辰狀元 contains a complete four-pillar chart followed by Bingchen and “first graduate.” Bingchen is most plausibly the examination year; “year” is supplied in brackets. It is not silently treated as a fifth natal pillar or a replacement hour. In the opening sentence 丁巳 is read as Ding and Ji, the Wealth/Officer pair within Wu, rather than a Dingsi pillar.

[^86]: **Separate pairings, not one four-stem mixture.** Geng/Wu and Xin/Ji are rendered as paired influences in the discussion of distinct Killings–Seal uses; S’s 巳 is normalized to Ji where that pairing requires it. The following Jiwei-hour case distinguishes a Killings arrangement from the Wu–Gui transformation obstructed by Ji’s jealousy. Its warning against excessive restraint remains explicit.

[^87]: **The ninth-month exceptions and a retained northward contradiction.** The Guihai discussion varies with hour and the presence of Earth, Fire, or Jia. Those qualifications are retained despite the compressed wording. The next verse praises northward travel with red and yellow, but its commentary says much original Fire and Earth makes the north inauspicious. Both S and G603 transmit 不吉; the translation does not reverse the negation merely to harmonize verse and prose.

[^88]: **Punishment Combination’s differing branch warnings.** The final verse avoids Shen and Wu, while its commentary also permits Wu and Xu under particular conditions and separately avoids repeated Earth and Shen. The differences remain visible. The two closing examples report withdrawal from office in Shen luck, not death. The reader should not substitute a fatal prediction for the actual wording 休致 and 休官.

[^89]: **A new emphasis, not a replacement elemental table.** The source announces particular attention to nayin. Its headings also employ the “true” Five Phases associated with stem combinations: Jia/Ji Earth, Yi/Geng Metal, Bing/Xin Water, Ding/Ren Wood, Wu/Gui Fire. The ensuing commentary nevertheless continues to use ordinary stem phases and branch phases as well. These frames are kept distinct rather than forcing every statement into only one. “The six Jia,” “six Yi,” and so forth mean the six sexagenary pairs sharing that stem; they do not by themselves specify the day rather than birth year. The English umbrella heading for this sequence, and the later heading separating the general Five-Phase judgments, are editorial.

[^90]: **Two readings supported by the earlier parallel.** **W** denotes the *Wuxing jingji* transcription, [revision 10494296](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=10494296), whose volumes four and five preserve related verses and explanations. In the Cloudy-River paragraph it supports reading “scant Wealth, pure distinction; the discourse says…” rather than inventing a work titled *Discourse on Pure Distinction* from 清貴論. In Blessing and Lu Return to the Root, W has 甲己 where S and G603 have 甲乙; “[Ji]” is adopted, fitting the Earth-combination argument. W is a parallel textual witness, not another Siku edition or evidence that every word in it is superior.

[^91]: **Stem, branch, and nayin in one example.** Jiachen and Jiaxu here contain Wood in the stem, Earth in the branch, and Fire in the nayin. Dingsi and Jiyou supply Earth nayin at Metal’s Birth and flourishing positions. These overlapping affiliations explain why the passage cannot be translated as a single list of fixed physical substances. The quoted Embryo/parents language remains the source’s analogy.

[^92]: **Tomb and two lost glyphs.** S’s 木中宿火 in the Jiaxu explanation is read with W’s 墓中宿火, Fire lodged within the Tomb; the supplied reading is bracketed. S ends the next verse with two incomplete character-composition signs, ⿰⿰. G603 supplies 摩羅; W gives the decompositions (土摩)(土羅). These witnesses establish that text is missing in S, but do not fully settle its lexical interpretation. “[Afflictions]” is a contextual, provisional rendering of the difficult expression, not a claim that the original glyphs have been securely reconstructed.

[^93]: **Traditional branch titles and true Metal.** Following Chief 從魁 is You, Great Auspiciousness 大吉 Chou, Messenger 傳送 Shen, and Taiyi 太乙 Si. They are branch titles in these formulas. “Yi is yin Metal and generates Water; mother and child are close” punctuates the generation argument without making Water the mother of Metal. The later warning against Bing and Ding returns to ordinary Fire controlling Metal.

[^94]: **Bing Water and Bing Fire coexist in the transmitted explanation.** The heading gives true Water, while the commentary explicitly speaks of Bing Fire, Jia/Ji Earth, and Metal generating Bing Water. The translation preserves those transitions. S’s “controlling Wealth and generating Officer” is retained against G603’s “flourishing Wealth and generating Officer.” The opening “no further Bing” supplies the natural ellipsis, not the impossible claim that a Bing-affiliated person lacks their own defining stem.

[^95]: **One Wood and one Metal.** S asks for one Metal to assist the qi; G603 has an altered phrase involving an Yi position and unmixed qi. S controls the translation here. In the following Bingyin/Jiayin example, Fire and Water are nayin affiliations; the apparently identical Yin branch does not make the two pillars function identically.

[^96]: **Food God can injure the actual source of office.** Wu is Bing’s Food God by the ordinary same-polarity generation relationship, yet strong Earth may destroy Water, which supplies Bing’s Officer. The passage is explaining that reversal. The verse places Wu above Shen or Zi while the commentary also discusses Zi and Wu as flourishing Earth positions; their difference is retained.

[^97]: **Ding’s difficult heading and the Water sequence.** 火父木成數三 is rendered close to its wording, with Wood as Fire’s father and three called the completing number. This is not silently changed to the usual generating/completing-number table. The opening uses Ding’s true-Wood relationship to Wu/Gui Fire; the later section returns to ordinary Ding Fire, Water Officer, Metal Wealth, and Wood Seal. Its verse’s Food-God label remains as transmitted even though the commentary discusses Metal generating Officer.

[^98]: **Named stems need appropriate ground.** The Ren/Hai verse and its gloss distinguish a stem appearing from Water actually obtaining its proper region. The final Ding warning likewise considers Water and Earth sharing Shen’s Birth position: favorable-sounding Birth does not decide which influence prevails. These are relative configurations, not independent illness predictions.

[^99]: **Wu’s true Fire and ordinary Earth.** The heading’s Fire number is not substituted for ordinary Wu Earth in the next explanations. “The child generates its father” is the source’s compressed assertion and is retained rather than rewritten as an ordinary one-directional generation cycle. The Wuyin/Wushen and Guihai example then explicitly seeks Wood to regulate strong Earth and Water to nourish that Wood.

[^100]: **A conjectural Wood correction.** S and G603 call Jia 陽火, yang Fire, in a paragraph where Jia injures Wu, Water strengthens Jia, and Geng Metal restrains it. “[Wood]” is a contextual emendation, not an attested agreement between those witnesses. It restores the relationship explicitly described on both sides of the damaged phrase. The note records the original reading so that the repair is not mistaken for a secure critical-text finding.

[^101]: **Two supported repairs in Ji’s true-Earth sequence.** S has 一庚, “one Geng,” where G603 has 乙庚, Yi and Geng; the latter is adopted in brackets, fitting the Metal child of Earth and the immediately following substitution rules. In Completion Encloses Combined Lu, S calls the unwanted influence “true Fire” and also calls Bing/Xin true Fire. G603 corrects the commentary to true Water but leaves the verse unchanged; W has true Water in both. The translation adopts “[Water]” in both places. These repairs are textually supported, not merely imposed from a preferred modern system.

[^102]: **Implicit generation and auspicious branch names.** The Ji paragraph explicitly allows Water’s ground to generate Water without visible Water in the stems. This is an argument about implicit relationships, not a missing character to be supplied. Its short subtitle ends with strengthening Metal, although the main explanation culminates in nourishing Jia; that compressed wording is retained. Great and Lesser Auspiciousness in the Geng passage are Chou and Wei. “Generation by a child” is the transmitted phrase; the full explanation works through Fire generating Earth and Earth assisting Metal rather than replacing the ordinary generation cycle with its reverse.

[^103]: **Geng’s Fire use and the time of generation.** “Fire is generated in the third month” is retained as the source’s wording, not standardized into a claim that Fire’s ordinary Birth stage is Chen. The final Geng case uses Jiashen’s Water nayin: it is not a new assertion that ordinary Jia or Shen alone belongs to Water. Luoluzi’s “encountering, yet not encountering” means that the named indicator has appeared without obtaining effective use.

[^104]: **Heaven’s One and an uncertain graph.** S and G603 print 天乙生水, Tianyi generates Water. In the numerical context, the translation conjecturally reads 天一生水, “Heaven’s One generates Water,” and brackets “One.” This is not presented as an attested agreement between those two witnesses. Heavenly Chief 天魁 denotes Xu and Merit Officer 功曹 Yin in the source’s own explanation. The later Xin/Fire discussion returns to ordinary Xin Metal, despite the true-Water heading.

[^105]: **Substitution pairs are not impossible sexagenary pillars.** 丙酉 in S, G603, and W cannot be a normal Bing–You pillar, since its stem and branch have opposite parity. Here it follows the explicit instruction that You substitutes for absent Xin when Bing is present. It is therefore rendered “Bing with You,” parallel to “Xin with Si,” rather than assigned an invented place in the sixty-pair cycle. The numerical phrase 受己之數 is retained as receiving Ji’s number; W’s variant involving receiving qi is not silently substituted.

[^106]: **The Ren/Jihai Water warning.** S and G603’s 忽逢水數, “suddenly meeting Water numbers,” contradicts the ensuing explanation that more Water strengthens the Wood which damages Officer Earth. W has 勿逢水數, “do not meet Water numbers,” adopted as “[Let no]” in the verse. G603 and W also support 上, “above,” where S has 土 in 下殘切土; the translation brackets the correction. In the next paragraph, S’s 䄂禄 is read as 福祿, blessing and Lu, supported by G603. Jihai’s Wood is its nayin, while Ji Earth is the stem: the two levels must remain distinct.

[^107]: **Ren’s true-Wood heading does not erase its ordinary Water role.** The Fire–Earth mediation and the Wu stem-ghost explanation use ordinary Ren Water and Earth Officer/Killings. The compilation moves between these and the earlier true-number model. The explicit Jia-Wood rescue is preserved, without treating the heading as a command to replace every later reference to Ren with Wood.

[^108]: **Gui’s three poems use different reference frames.** The first uses true Gui Fire and the Jia/Ji Earth combination; the next uses ordinary Gui Water gathering with Hai and Zi; the third distinguishes Wu Officer from Ji Killings. Messenger is Shen, conventionally named in the text’s branch-title scheme. The assertion that it is Jia/Ji’s birthplace is retained within the true-Earth argument, not recast as Jia Wood’s ordinary Birth. The thousand measures of Lu are poetic abundance, not a salary calculation.

[^109]: **Guihai and Xinhai are not interchangeable Hai positions.** Guihai supplies Water nayin at Wood’s Birth; Xinhai supplies Metal nayin there. This explains why one nourishes while the other can injure. The Xinhai commentary explicitly warns that the Fire–Wood remedy would itself be unsuitable without the condition it remedies. “Birth meeting Birth” preserves the source’s language of generation within a Birth position, not two additional independently calculated stages.

[^110]: **Water correction and a compressed generating chain.** In Ghost Qi at Arrival at Office, S’s 氷, ice, is read as 水, Water, supported by G603. The following Fire paragraph lets Water generate Wood, Wood generate Fire, and then Metal restrain Wood and generate Fire’s Officer. The final link is compressed in S; it is retained without inserting an unprinted explanatory sentence into the source commentary. “Leaving the prior form and returning to the origin” translates 脫體還元 within this changing sequence.

[^111]: **Fire at its birthplace can still be injured.** Jiayin’s Water nayin, not its ordinary Wood stem alone, supplies the adverse encounter. The text simultaneously permits a little Wood to sustain Fire and warns against more than the needed amount. The physiological and family consequences in these verses remain historical judgments, not clinical claims or advice concerning an actual family.

[^112]: **Five Woods and robust qi.** 五木成煙 is retained as the transmitted title; the body does not enumerate five separate Wood pillars, and none is invented. Literary Melody 文曲 is a technical/literary designation here, not a modern astronomical identification. G603 supports 己土, Ji Earth, and 壯實, robust and substantial, where S has 巳土 and 北實. The latter repair is bracketed. The preceding praise of strong Earth with Fire is qualified by the following warning against excess; both are translated.

[^113]: **Rescue does not necessarily restore the whole configuration.** 救助减福 allows relief while denying that the underlying arrangement has become fully auspicious. The next quotation distinguishes Water and Earth in their weakened places, ending with Guiyou as an exception. The compression and apparent tension are retained rather than turning every mention of Bathing or Defeat into one universal adverse rule.

[^114]: **Rooted strength versus an injured Birth position.** The Metal paragraph first permits flourishing Fire with sufficiently strong Metal, then gives Yisi Fire at Metal’s Birth as an injurious case. Yisi’s Fire is nayin. These are different conditions, not a contradiction solved by changing the phase assigned to Yisi. The heading Heavenly Theft is retained as a named judgment.

[^115]: **Two repairs in the final Water sequence.** S’s 見火却生火, “seeing Fire then generates Fire,” is read as 見木卻生火, “Wood encountered then generates Fire,” supported by G603 and W. S and G603 later have 若無水火, “without Water and Fire,” while W has 若無木火, “without Wood and Fire,” agreeing with the verse. “[Wood]” is therefore adopted in both places, with the differing witness support made explicit. The opening statement that Wood can be useful is preserved: the later danger arises through the additional Fire and its injury to Metal, Water’s source.

[^116]: **The closing examples retain their layered affiliations.** Wushen and Wuyin are Earth nayin at Water’s Birth and Sickness respectively. The last verse names Bing on Chen or Xu; the commentary gives both Bingchen and Bingxu even though their nayin differ. They are retained, not forced into a single nayin-only explanation at the expense of stem Fire and branch Earth. The juan’s repeated concern is whether generation, restraint, and mediation preserve the useful relationship; neither an auspicious name nor a strong phase is independently sufficient.
