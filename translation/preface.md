# Sanming Tonghui · Siku Editorial Preface
## A Comprehensive Gathering of the Three Fates

*The notice below is the Siku editors’ assessment, not a preface by Wan Minying. Its judgments and first-person voice belong to those editors. The Chinese notice is preserved at the beginning of the repository’s first source file.*[^01]

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATED SIKU PREFACE -->
<!-- source: L13-L18 -->
**Imperially Commissioned Siku Quanshu — Masters Division, VII**

**Sanming Tonghui — Techniques of Calculation, V**

〈Books of Destiny and Physiognomy.〉

## Editorial Notice
### 提要

<!-- source: L20-L20 -->
We, your servants, respectfully submit the following assessment.[^02]

The *Sanming Tonghui* comprises twelve juan. The original text does not give its author’s personal name; at the head of the work it says only, “Written by Yuwu, the Mountain Recluse.”[^03] The “Bibliographical Treatise” of the *History of the Ming* lists a *Sanming Huitong* in twelve juan by Wan Minyu. Its number of juan agrees with this copy, the slight difference being that *tonghui* appears as *huitong*.[^04] We have now examined the widely circulated *Xingxue dacheng*, which treats specifically of the Five Planets: it was written by Wan Minying and has already been entered in this catalogue. This work, which treats specifically of Ziping, is likewise from Minying’s hand.[^05] The “Bibliographical Treatise” apparently confused Minying with Minyu because of the sobriquet “Yuwu, the Mountain Recluse”; the two characters *tong* and *hui* were also transposed in copying. Hence the discrepancy: the entry in fact refers to this very book.

For more than two hundred years, from the Ming onward, those who discuss stellar fate-calculation have all treated this work as their comprehensive collection: scarcely a household is without a copy. Yet the Eight-Character charts of officeholders recorded in it often extend to men of the late Ming. These were probably interpolated in later commercial printings, which no longer preserve the original text unchanged.[^06]

Nevertheless, in elucidating the methods bequeathed by Ziping, it gathers the words of many authorities and grasps their essentials: the names and meanings of Officer, Seal, Wealth, Lu, Food God, and Hurting Officer; the relative weight of the useful spirit; and the good and ill fortune associated with the various spirits and *sha*.[^07] For this reason practitioners have continually used it. It contains, in short, matter that cannot lightly be cast aside.

Yet its arguments favor Direct Officer, Direct Seal, and Direct Wealth, without recognizing that Indirect Officer, Indirect Seal, and Indirect Wealth can also lend their strength. It knows that Food God can bring forth refinement, but not that Hurting Officer can likewise yield extraordinary distinction. Here its view is partial and rigid, its understanding not fully rounded or complete.[^08]

Moreover, its discussions of the Conception Origin and similar matters often fail when applied in the present day. Those who study fate need only grasp its broad purport and adapt it with judgment; that is sufficient.[^09]

As for the passages it quotes from Luoluzi, their wording differs markedly from that of the complete text now recovered from the *Yongle dadian*. At that time, however, the text was preserved out of public reach, and readers outside had no means of consulting it. He was consequently misled into trusting a spuriously attributed version. This can hardly be held against him.[^10][^11]

Respectfully collated and submitted in the ninth month of the forty-fourth year of Qianlong [1779].[^12]

**Chief Compilers:** your servants Ji Yun, Lu Xixiong, and Sun Shiyi.

**Chief Collator:** your servant Lufei Chi.[^13]

## Translator’s Endnotes

[^01]: **Text, witnesses, and scope.** The controlling text, **S**, is the region marked `SIKU PREFACE` in [source/juan-01.md](../source/juan-01.md), corresponding to [Wikisource revision 657391](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=657391). This is the preface’s own revision, not revision 657365, which supplies the first juan proper. The header checksum identifies the complete combined source file. **C** is the related catalogue entry in the [*Siku Quanshu zongmu tiyao*, juan 109, revision 2272698](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=2272698). **M** is the [*Ming shi*, juan 98, revision 2594716](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=2594716); **Q** is the [*Qing shi gao*, juan 320, revision 2550999](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?oldid=2550999). These are consulted transcriptions of primary texts, not four independent editions of *Sanming Tonghui*. No fresh facsimile collation is claimed. S governs the wording, including passages absent from C. The classification, dated submission, and all four signatories are translated; no authorial preface from a different edition has been substituted or appended.

[^02]: **A notice addressed to the throne.** 提要, *tiyao*, is an editorial account of a book’s contents, provenance, and merits. It is called a “preface” here for its place before the work, not because the compiler wrote it. 臣等謹案 is the editors’ formal address as servants of the emperor. The repeated 臣 in the signatures has the same function. The source’s classification markers 七 and 五 are reproduced as VII and V; they are not counts of the book’s juan or chapters. “Techniques of Calculation” follows the earlier translations’ rendering of 術數 and does not mean modern mathematics alone.

[^03]: **The name on the original title.** 育吾山人, Yuwu Shanren, is rendered “Yuwu, the Mountain Recluse”; “Mountain Recluse” is a self-designation, not an additional personal name or proof of a particular residence. C explicitly connects 育吾 with Wan Minying. The notice’s assertion that the original lacked a personal name concerns the copy or textual state the editors are describing. It does not require deleting the attribution to 萬民英 already printed in the repository’s juan headings.

[^04]: **Preserve the bibliographical discrepancy, including its limits.** S deliberately contrasts 萬民育, Wan Minyu, with 萬民英, Wan Minying, and 三命會通, *Sanming Huitong*, with 三命通會, *Sanming Tonghui*. Correcting these forms in the English would destroy the editors’ argument. C reports the same errors. M’s consulted transcription, however, already reads 萬民英《三命會通》十二卷: the inverted title remains, but the personal name is Minying, not Minyu. The translation therefore preserves what the notice reports without claiming that every received text of the *Ming History* has that name error. Establishing which printing or later editorial intervention accounts for the discrepancy would require further collation.

[^05]: **Two fields of practice.** *Xingxue dacheng* 星學大成 means approximately “Great Compendium of Stellar Studies.” 五星 here denotes the Five Planets, not the Five Phases as such. 子平, Ziping, names the fate-calculation tradition discussed in the following juan, rather than a second author named in this sentence. The editors contrast two works by their principal subject. Their “already entered in this catalogue” points to an entry elsewhere in the Siku catalogue, not to an earlier section of *Sanming Tonghui*. The contrasting *Xingxue dacheng* entry is also in C.

[^06]: **Circulation and later additions.** “More than two hundred years” and “scarcely a household” are the editors’ historical estimate and rhetoric, not an independently established printing chronology or readership count. C omits the explicit two-hundred-year phrase; S retains it. 明季 means the late or final Ming, and 坊刻 means commercial printings. 蓋 marks the explanation of interpolation as an inference. The notice does not identify every added chart, so it cannot by itself justify removing particular cases from [juan eight](juan-08.md) or [juan nine](juan-09.md). Those transmitted records remain in the translation.

[^07]: **The six technical names.** 官、印、財、祿、食、傷 are rendered individually as Officer, Seal, Wealth, Lu, Food God, and Hurting Officer. 用神 remains “useful spirit”: the influence employed in a particular analysis, whose strength and relative importance must be weighed. It is not silently reduced to a single universally favorable “element.” 神煞 includes the named spirits and *sha* treated especially in [juan three](juan-03.md); not every occurrence of 煞 elsewhere in the book denotes the same kind of indicator.

[^08]: **The editors’ criticism is not the book’s own conclusion.** The categorical “without recognizing” and “but not” are retained, rather than softened into a modern interpretation of what the editors ought to have meant. Their charge is difficult to reconcile with the transmitted contents. [Juan five](juan-05.md) has separate discussions of Indirect Officer (§3), Indirect Wealth (§5), Inverted Food or Indirect Seal (§8), and Hurting Officer (§10), including circumstances in which these bring distinction. [Juan twelve](juan-12.md), especially the *Rhapsody on the True Treasure* (§2), likewise describes powerful and prestigious configurations involving these influences. The notice is therefore evidence of the Qing editors’ assessment, not a sufficient description of everything the compilation teaches. 吐秀, “bring forth refinement,” and 出奇, “yield extraordinary distinction,” retain its contrast between the beneficial workings of Food God and Hurting Officer without supplying a new rule for either.

[^09]: **Conception Origin, “today,” and adaptable judgment.** 胎元 is the Conception Origin discussed in [juan two, §15](juan-02.md#15-on-the-conception-origin), not the Day Master. 今日 refers to the editors’ own time. Their statement that the method often fails is preserved as their assessment, not treated as a modern empirical test; equally, their praise elsewhere is not validation of predictive efficacy. 變通 means adapting an understanding to differing circumstances, not blindly repeating formulas. The instruction to grasp the book’s broad purport does not authorize silently rewriting its particular doctrines or resolving every disagreement between its authorities.

[^10]: **Luoluzi and the recovered text.** The *Yongle dadian* 永樂大典 is the Yongle encyclopaedia. “Recovered” renders 輯: the editors are speaking of assembling a text from the collection, not obtaining its author’s autograph. C separately describes an annotated text attributed to Xu Ziping and another transmitted under the monk Tanying’s name, incorporating other commentators. The notice does not identify a unique original recension. Its judgment that the version used here is 依託, spuriously attributed, is translated as an editorial allegation, not established anew by this translation. The Luoluzi material and its competing explanations remain fully represented in [juan eleven](juan-11.md), particularly §§4–5. 秘冊 and 外間 contrast restricted preservation with access outside it; they do not specify an occult initiation or a particular library in this sentence.

[^11]: **A small, supported repair.** S prints 外間末由窺見, with 末, “end,” where C has 未, “not yet/not.” The translation follows 未由, “had no means of,” supported by C and the sentence’s explanation of why the earlier compiler could not consult the text. The Chinese source file remains unchanged. Orthographic forms such as 巻 for 卷 and contextually clear 己 for 已 are read normally; the disputed personal name and reversed book title, by contrast, are deliberately retained because they are part of the argument.

[^12]: **Submission date, not a modern month.** 乾隆四十四年九月 is the ninth month of Qianlong 44, corresponding to the regnal year 1779. The supplied Gregorian year appears in brackets. The ninth month is a Chinese calendrical month, not necessarily September; no day is given. 恭校上 states that the text was respectfully collated and submitted to the throne. It is not a date of composition for the Ming work itself, nor proof that every surviving copy was physically written in that month.

[^13]: **Four signatories and a compound surname.** 總纂官 names the Chief Compilers: 紀昀, Ji Yun; 陸錫熊, Lu Xixiong; and 孫士毅, Sun Shiyi. 總校官 names the Chief Collator: 陸費墀, Lufei Chi. The spacing of the latter name must not split it into several people or turn 費 into the first part of a two-character given name. Q explicitly states 陸費為複姓, “Lufei is a compound surname,” and identifies his role in the Siku office. These are the editorial signatories, not four additional authors of the Ming treatise.
